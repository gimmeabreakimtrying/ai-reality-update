Artificially Unintelligent

How Artificial Intelligence Actually Hurt Society (So Far)

by Brodie Lewis (reality update)

October 19, 2026

Back in early 2023 this page warned that AI could make existing problems worse. Three years later the warnings weren’t pure fiction — they just played out messier and slower than the cartoon version.

Bias is still a real problem. AI hiring tools have repeatedly shown demographic skews. NYC bias audits found groups falling under fairness thresholds. ChatGPT and other models have been caught applying caste bias in India, gender and racial stereotypes in outputs, and hiring filters that disadvantage certain applicants even when resumes are identical. Companies keep promising fixes. The issues keep showing up in court and in studies.

Job displacement is happening, but concentrated. Entry-level and junior roles in software, customer service, and other AI-exposed work have taken measurable hits. Stanford data and others show young workers in those fields lagging peers. Companies have cited AI in over 100,000 announced cuts in 2026 so far. The IMF talks about roughly 40% of jobs worldwide being augmented, changed, or eliminated over time. It is not the full “everyone is unemployed tomorrow” scenario, but it is already reshaping who gets hired first.

Surveillance and control expanded exactly as predicted. Always-listening AI wearables from Apple, Meta, and startups are out. Workplace monitoring software tracks keystrokes, tone, and activity. AI cameras track movement patterns. Governments and companies use AI for social media analysis and monitoring. Privacy laws are struggling to keep up.

The original article was blunt satire. Reality is more boring and more widespread: bias that doesn’t go away, junior jobs getting squeezed, and more cameras and microphones listening. Not the end of the world. Also not nothing.

Artificially Unintelligent Part 2

AI Didn’t Destroy Every Local Business, But It Squeezed a Lot of Them

by Brodie Lewis (reality update)

October 19, 2026

The 2023 version claimed robot chefs had already wiped out a family restaurant. That specific story was made up. The broader pattern was not.

Automation and AI tools have pressured businesses that rely on routine human labor. Customer service roles, data entry, basic content work, and some retail and service jobs have seen reduced hiring or cuts. Companies report efficiency gains. Workers report fewer entry points. The “robot does it cheaper and never takes a break” dynamic is real in call centers, warehouses, and back-office work even if it hasn’t replaced every cook yet.

Wealth concentration around the companies that own the models and the data is also visible. A handful of large tech firms control the infrastructure most people now use daily. Smaller operators and workers without the capital or skills to adapt get squeezed. This is not a clean dystopia. It is the same old pattern of technology favoring people who already have advantages, just faster.

The family restaurant story was exaggeration for effect. The part about AI making some work cheaper to automate and leaving people behind is the part that kept happening.

Artificially Unintelligent Part 3

AI Has Not Fully Taken Over, But It Is In Charge of More Than It Used to Be

by Brodie Lewis (reality update)

October 19, 2026

"The development of full artificial intelligence could spell the end of the human race.... It would take off on its own, and re-design itself at an ever increasing rate. Humans, who are limited by slow biological evolution, couldn't compete, and would be superseded." — Stephen Hawking, BBC, 2014

That quote is still being passed around in 2026. AI lab leaders now publicly talk about slowing capability advances because of control and misuse risks. Autonomous systems exist in limited military and commercial contexts. Full takeover has not happened. Gradual integration into decision systems has.

Recommendation algorithms already shape a large share of what people see, buy, and argue about. AI assists or drafts in healthcare notes, financial tools, hiring screens, and content moderation. Tech companies with the best models hold significant leverage over how information and services are delivered. Workplace AI monitors performance. Wearables listen more often. None of this is science fiction anymore. It is ordinary infrastructure that keeps expanding.

The risk of systems making consequential decisions with less human oversight is real and debated. So is the risk that the people who build and own the systems end up with more power over everyone else. Hawking’s warning was about what happens if the systems get good enough and poorly aligned. We are not there yet. We are closer than we were in 2023, and the people building it are arguing about how fast to go.

The original ending said the dark age had already started. It hadn’t. What did start is the steady expansion of AI into more decisions, more monitoring, and more leverage for the companies that control it. That part was directionally right.
